# Leafnote — Research

## Research status and scope

This document archives ongoing exploratory research for Leafnote. The project
examines how plant owners and temporary caregivers make watering decisions,
retain care context, and transfer that context when care changes hands.

**Status:** Early exploration. Very small sample. Not validated.

### Self-analysis insights

*Preliminary interpretation of the journey maps above. Not interview evidence.*

1. **Care relied on rough judgment.** The author did not follow precise care
   schedules or keep detailed information about each plant. Instead, they relied
   on vague memory, visual cues, and simple personal rules to decide when and
   how much to water.

2. **Each action left no continuous history.** Watering was treated as an
   isolated task. Without a record of what had happened, the next decision
   depended on incomplete recollection.

3. **Handoff transferred the task, but not the responsibility.** A temporary
   caregiver could perform the watering, but the owner still had to organize
   instructions, remember when care was needed, send repeated reminders, and
   follow up without seeing the plants' current condition.

4. **Repeated uncertainty created a low-level mental burden.** While no
   individual step felt especially difficult, the combined uncertainty kept the
   experience between neutral and mildly frustrated, especially when the owner
   was away.

> *"The task could be delegated, but the context and mental responsibility
> could not."*

## Initial assumptions

*Preliminary assumptions derived from researcher self-analysis on 2026-08-27.
Not tested against interview data. Not validated.*

1. Casual plant owners may rely on rough judgment rather than a consistent care
   system.
2. Without a visible care history, owners may feel uncertain about what has
   already been done.
3. When care changes hands, instructions may transfer more easily than the
   context behind them.
4. Even after delegating the task, owners may continue carrying the planning
   and follow-up responsibility.

## Interview 01: plant owner

*Conducted 2026-08-28 to 2026-08-31. Exploratory. n = 1.*

*Independent participant. Not the researcher. Not connected to Interview 02.
Describes this participant's own plants and their own travel handoff
arrangements.*

### Q1. How many plants do you currently have, and who usually cares for them?

**Response (raw evidence):**

The participant had three houseplants: two succulents and one lucky bamboo.

Their later responses indicated that they were familiar with the plants'
everyday routines and normally made watering decisions themselves.

**Unanswered / not established:**

- Whether anyone else regularly shares the care responsibility

---

### Q2. Think back to the most recent time you watered your plants. What started the process?

**Response (raw evidence):**

The participant remembered watering the plants approximately one week earlier.

They did not describe beginning from a scheduled reminder. Instead, watering
was triggered by visible conditions:

- The succulent leaves had become soft
- The lucky bamboo's container had run out of water

The participant described moving directly from noticing these conditions to
watering the plants.

---

### Q3. How did you decide which plants needed water?

**Response (raw evidence):**

The participant used different visible cues for the two types of plants.

For the succulents, soft leaves indicated that watering might be needed. For
the lucky bamboo, the participant checked whether water remained in its
container.

**Researcher notes (preliminary):**

The participant did not describe treating all plants according to one shared
schedule. Each plant type had a simple condition that triggered care.

---

### Q4. How did you decide how much water to give each plant?

**Response (raw evidence):**

The watering method differed by plant type.

For the succulents, the participant watered them several times until the soil
had been thoroughly watered. For the lucky bamboo, they added enough water to
ensure that water remained in the container.

The participant did not describe using precise measurements. The stopping
point was determined by the plant and container setup.

---

### Q5. After watering, how did you know when the plants needed water again?

**Response (raw evidence):**

The participant continued monitoring the same visible conditions:

- Whether the succulent leaves had become soft
- Whether the lucky bamboo's container had run out of water

They checked the succulents' leaves every few days and periodically looked at
the water level of the lucky bamboo.

**Researcher notes (preliminary):**

The participant relied on recurring observation rather than remembering a
fixed next watering date.

---

### Q6. Think back to the most recent time you were away from home. How did you arrange care for your plants?

**Response (raw evidence):**

The participant used different travel arrangements for different plants.

They watered the succulents before leaving and planned to water them again
after returning because the succulents did not require frequent watering.

The lucky bamboo required more continuous access to water, so the participant
left it at another person's home while away.

**Researcher notes (preliminary):**

The participant did not transfer care for every plant. The handoff was limited
to the lucky bamboo — the plant whose needs were less compatible with the
travel period, as described by the participant.

**Unanswered / not established:**

- What instructions were given to the temporary caregiver
- Who decided when additional water was needed
- How the plant's condition was communicated during the trip

*Later addressed in [Interview 01 follow-up Q5](#follow-up-q5-think-back-to-the-most-recent-time-you-were-away-from-home-how-did-you-arrange-care-for-your-plants) (same participant). Original response above is preserved.*

---

They mentioned the possibility of disease, unexplained leaf loss, and

**Researcher notes (preliminary):**

#### Interview 01 follow-up — preliminary observations

*Interview notes associated with this participant. Not validated findings.
Not product requirements.*

1. **Care followed a check-and-respond routine rather than a fixed schedule.**
   The participant did not calculate precise watering dates. Instead, they
   remembered to check the plants periodically and watered selectively when
   familiar signs appeared.

2. **Simple personal rules substituted for detailed plant-care knowledge.**
   The participant did not measure water or maintain detailed care
   instructions. They used a small number of plant-specific rules to make
   routine decisions manageable.

3. **Travel care was selective and relied on trust.** The participant watered
   the succulents before leaving and handed over only the lucky bamboo, with
   one simple instruction, and did not send reminders or maintain visibility.

4. **Uncertainty emerged when familiar care cues were no longer sufficient.**
   Routine watering appeared manageable because of recognizable visual rules.
   Greater uncertainty appeared with abnormal conditions. In the yellow-leaf
   episode, the difficulty was not accessing information, but knowing whether
   the explanation and treatment were appropriate.

## Interview 02: temporary caregiver

*Conducted 2026-08-28 to 2026-08-31. Exploratory. n = 1.*

*Participant previously cared for the researcher's plants during a separate
handoff. Not connected to Interview 01. Questions refer to the researcher's
plants ("my plants"), not Interview 01 participant's plants.*

### Q1. When I asked you to water my plants, what did you understand your responsibilities to be?

**Response (raw evidence):**

The participant understood that the task involved more than simply adding water.
They needed to:

- Identify which plants required care
- Understand how frequently each one should be watered
- Determine when enough water had been given

The watering method also depended on the pot setup. A pot with a tray provided
a visible stopping point, while a pot without one required more judgment. If
the soil was very dry, water could drain away before being fully absorbed,
making it difficult to tell whether the plant had been watered thoroughly. The
participant also needed to decide where to water each plant in order to manage
the runoff.

---

### Q2. What instructions do you remember receiving from me?

**Response (raw evidence):**

The participant remembered receiving instructions about which plants needed
water and a general idea of how much water to provide.

However, the instructions did not clearly explain:

- How to adjust the watering method for different pot setups
- How to tell when the soil had absorbed enough water

Pots with trays were relatively straightforward because the tray level
provided a visible cue. Pots without trays created more uncertainty,
particularly when dry soil allowed water to run through without being fully
absorbed.

When this became relevant, the participant asked whether individual pots would
leak and where they should be watered.

---

### Q3. Think back to the first time you watered the plants. What did you do?

**Response (raw evidence):**

The participant relied primarily on the owner's instructions to identify which
plants needed water and how much water to use.

The amount was not measured precisely. The participant described using an
approximate sense of whether the plant had received enough water.

---

### Q4. Were there any moments when you had to decide what to do on your own?

**Response (raw evidence):**

The participant followed the owner's instructions to understand which plants
needed water and their general watering frequency. However, they still needed
to interpret how to carry out the task in the moment.

They considered:

- Whether a pot would leak
- Where the plant should be watered
- Soil saturation as a stopping cue rather than measuring a precise amount

For later watering, they also checked the soil moisture and whether the plant
appeared dry.

When they could not determine a practical detail — such as whether a pot would
leak — they contacted the owner for clarification rather than making the
decision entirely on their own.

---

### Q5. After the first watering, how did you know when the plants needed to be watered again?

**Response (raw evidence):**

The participant used two sources of information:

- The watering frequency provided by the owner
- Occasional checks of the soil moisture and whether the plant appeared dry

**Researcher notes (preliminary):**

The participant did not rely only on a fixed schedule, but also used visible
and physical plant conditions to decide whether watering was needed.

---

### Q6. What did you do after you finished watering?

**Response (raw evidence):**

After watering, the participant returned the plants to their original locations
and informed the owner that the task had been completed.

**Researcher notes (preliminary):**

The update confirmed completion, but it did not necessarily include a
detailed record of the amount of water used or the condition of each plant.

---

### Q7. If I had been away for longer, what would have become more difficult?

**Response (raw evidence):**

The participant thought it would become difficult to remember how often each
plant needed to be watered.

They suggested that a reminder — from the owner or one they set themselves —
would make repeated care easier.

They also expressed uncertainty about evaluating the plants' longer-term health
because they did not fully understand the plants' condition or survival needs.

---

### Q8. If you cared for my plants again, what would make the experience easier?

**Response (raw evidence):**

The participant wanted clearer and more detailed instructions, including:

- How often each plant should be watered
- How different types of plants should be watered
- Practical information about how to carry out the task

They also suggested sending photos of the plants to the owner. The owner could
then help assess the plants' condition and advise whether the watering approach
should change.

The participant remained uncertain about whether they could independently
evaluate each plant's needs.

#### Interview 02 follow-up — preliminary observations

*Summarized with ChatGPT assistance and checked by the researcher. Interview
notes associated with this participant. Not validated findings. Not product
requirements.*

1. **Instructions did not eliminate the need for on-site judgment.** The owner
   provided basic information about which plants needed water and their
   general watering frequency. The caregiver still needed to interpret the pot
   setup, choose a watering location, observe the soil, and determine when
   enough water had been given. When essential context was missing, they
   contacted the owner.

2. **Observable cues guided the caregiver's actions.** The participant did not
   measure water by volume. They relied on visible conditions, such as
   saturated soil or collected water in a tray, to decide when to stop.

3. **Execution was delegated, but scheduling remained with the owner.** Once
   the watering method was understood, the participant felt relatively capable
   of carrying it out. They were concerned about forgetting when plants needed
   water again and expected the owner to maintain the schedule and provide
   reminders. Without reminders, they would rely on irregular checks and an
   approximate weekly routine.

4. **Routine updates and exceptions required different levels of
   communication.** A simple completion message was considered sufficient
   after routine watering. Frequent photos were not considered necessary. When
   a plant appeared abnormal, the participant wanted to send a photo and rely
   on the owner to assess its condition and advise what to do next.

## Contradictions and differences

*Preliminary observations. Not resolved. Subject to update.*

**Cue-based practice vs. frequency in instructions** *(independent sources)*

Interview 01 (independent plant owner) describes cue-based monitoring with no
fixed next watering date in their own routine.

Interview 02 (caregiver for the researcher's plants) used the researcher's
frequency guidance alongside physical checks when deciding whether to water
again.

These come from **separate handoff events**. No comparison between Interview 01
participant's practice and Interview 02 instructions is possible. The
parallel is observational only: one owner practices cue-based monitoring;
one caregiver received frequency guidance plus used physical checks.

**Independence vs. dependence**

Interview 02 describes needing to interpret instructions in the moment, but
contacting the owner rather than deciding alone on practical details (e.g.,
whether a pot would leak).

**Selective vs. comprehensive handoff**

Interview 01 describes transferring care for only one plant (lucky bamboo),
not all plants. Self-analysis describes asking a friend to look after plants
more broadly. The scope of handoff may vary by plant need and travel
duration.

**Thin records from both roles**

Self-analysis notes that each watering action left no continuous history.
Interview 02 describes a post-watering update that confirmed completion but not
condition or amount. Both point to limited care records, observed from
different roles.

**Different types of uncertainty**

Interview 01 surfaces uncertainty about abnormal conditions (disease,
yellowing). Interview 02 surfaces uncertainty about longer-term health
evaluation. These may be related but were described separately.

Follow-up (preliminary): Interview 01 adds a failed self-directed intervention
after an online search. Interview 02 adds that routine watering felt
relatively capable once the method was understood; the larger worry was
forgetting, and unusual conditions should go back to the owner with a photo.

**Check-all vs. notice-and-water** *(same owner, original vs. follow-up)*

Original Interview 01 Q2 described moving from noticing conditions to
watering. Follow-up describes checking all plants, then watering only those
that had reached a personal threshold. These are not treated as two people;
they are two write-ups of the same owner. The follow-up sequence is more
specific; the original wording is preserved.

**Owner lets go vs. caregiver wants the owner to keep the schedule**
*(independent handoffs)*

Interview 01 follow-up (owner, lucky bamboo only): no reminders; caregiver
expected to watch water level; owner chose trust over visibility.

Interview 02 follow-up (caregiver for the researcher's plants): owner should
remain responsible for remembering the watering schedule.

Self-analysis: the researcher kept reminding from afar.

These are different situations. Whether mental responsibility stays with the
owner appears **context-dependent** (handoff complexity, plant type, role).
Not resolved.

**Routine record vs. exception communication**

Interview 02 follow-up: a completion message was enough after routine
watering; frequent photos were not wanted; a photo was wanted if the plant
looked unusual.

Interview 01 follow-up: the travel handoff produced no shared record, and the
owner did not ask for ongoing updates.

Self-analysis: lack of a record for next time was a friction in solo care.

A detailed long-term history is **not clearly supported** by either interview
follow-up. Whether anyone needs one remains unresolved.

## Working interpretations (preliminary)

*Researcher synthesis after follow-up. Based on self-analysis plus Interviews
01 and 02 (same two participants). Not validated. Not product requirements.*

Rather than treating the conversations as binary validation, each initial
assumption was examined for whether it was refined, expanded, challenged, or
left unresolved. Insights that were not part of the original framing are
labeled separately.

### Types of relationship (definitions)

| Label | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Refined** | The original assumption stayed relevant, but evidence made it more specific, nuanced, or complete |
| **Narrowed** | The assumption appeared to apply to a smaller or more specific situation than originally expected |
| **Context-dependent** | Whether the assumption applied depended on role, familiarity, complexity, or situation |
| **Emergent insight** | A pattern appeared that was not in the initial assumptions |

### Revisiting initial assumptions

| Initial assumption | What the conversations showed (preliminary) | Revised understanding (working) | Change |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Casual plant owners may rely on rough judgment rather than a consistent care system | The plant owner used familiar cues such as soft leaves or an empty water container. The caregiver combined basic instructions with soil moisture and saturation. | Decisions were not simply rough or random. They relied on informal but recognizable cue-based rules rather than precise schedules or measurements. | Refined |
| 2. Without a visible care history, owners may feel uncertain about what has already been done | A simple completion message was considered sufficient for routine watering. Neither conversation clearly demonstrated a need for a detailed, long-term history. | A shared current status and clear next action may be more important than a complete care history. Whether anyone needs a detailed history remains unresolved. | Narrowed |
| 3. When care changes hands, instructions may transfer more easily than the context behind them | The caregiver received basic instructions but still needed watering methods, pot setups, stopping cues, and abnormal-condition guidance. Needed detail also changed with the number and variety of plants. | Instructions and context cannot be treated separately. Useful instructions need enough practical context to support action, and the required level of detail depends on handoff complexity. | Refined |
| 4. Even after delegating the task, owners may continue carrying the planning and follow-up responsibility | Interview 01 owner: one rule, no reminders, trust, local decisions for one plant. Interview 02 caregiver: owner should remember the schedule. Self-analysis: owner kept reminding. | Whether the owner keeps mental load depends on handoff type, plant complexity, and role. | Context-dependent |

### What was learned across perspectives (preliminary)

- **Care was cue-based, not precision-based.** Visible or physical signs were used rather than exact measurements.
- **Guidance needed to be simple but actionable.** Caregivers still needed practical, observable instructions.
- **Handoff complexity shaped support needs.** Leaving one plant with a single rule is a different situation from caring for several plants with different pot setups.
- **Routine care and exceptions required different responses.** Routine watering and abnormal conditions were described as different communication problems.

### Emergent insight (preliminary)

Routine care and abnormal conditions may require different forms of
communication. Routine watering in Interview 02 used a brief completion
update. When a plant appeared abnormal, the caregiver wanted a photo and the
owner's input; the owner (Interview 01) independently searched for an
explanation and tried a treatment without a clear way to know if it was
right.

### Reframed problem (working interpretation)

Plant-care handoffs can transfer the physical task without clearly
transferring the context and responsibility needed to manage it.

Temporary caregivers may be able to perform routine watering, but they still
need actionable cues for when to act, how to carry out the task, and when an
abnormal condition should be reported. As the number and variety of plants
increase, informal instructions and memory may become less reliable, leaving
the owner involved in scheduling and higher-level decisions from afar.

This is a working problem statement. It is not a validated user need.

### Design question (not a finding)

How might we help casual plant owners transfer essential care context and
clarify responsibility — while keeping routine tasks simple for temporary
caregivers?

## Open research questions

*Research questions — not product requirements.*

### Decision-making

- What visible and physical cues do plant owners rely on, and how consistent
  are they across plant types?
- How do owners decide "enough water" without measurements?
- How do pot and tray setups change the watering method and stopping point?

### History and memory

- For routine watering, is a completion message enough — and for whom?
- When, if ever, is a longer care history useful?
- Does the absence of history affect the owner's decisions, the caregiver's
  decisions, or both?

### Handoff and delegation

- What instructions do owners actually give vs. what caregivers need?
- When does an owner transfer immediate decision-making (Interview 01 lucky
  bamboo) vs. keep the schedule (Interview 02)?
- How does handoff complexity (one simple rule vs. several plants and pot
  setups) change what must be included in instructions?
- When do caregivers seek owner input vs. act independently?
- What makes a handoff selective (some plants only) vs. comprehensive?

### Uncertainty

- How do owners and caregivers handle abnormal plant conditions differently
  from routine watering?
- What is the boundary between “I can evaluate this” and “I need to ask the
  owner”?
- After an owner tries an online suggestion that produces no visible result,
  what do they do next?
- What counts as “unusual enough” for a caregiver to send a photo?

### Longer absences

- What breaks down first when care extends beyond a short trip: remembering
  frequency, judging health, or knowing when to escalate?
- Would reminders, photos, or a shared current status change the experience —
  and for which role?
